title: Patterns in Time
description: An introduction to music as information and the psychology of expectation.

If we want to understand how music works on a structural level, we have to start by forgetting about notes, chords, and scales for a moment. We need to start with first principles: the physical reality of sound, and the psychology of how we listen to it.

## 1. The Raw Material: Sound vs Information

Imagine the chaotic din of a crowded room, the rustle of leaves, or the crackle of a radio tuned between stations. This is sound—energy travelling through the air—but it is highly disorganised. Because it lacks structure, our brains process it simply as noise.

However, when a sound begins to repeat itself at a fixed, regular interval, a profound shift occurs. A dripping tap or a steady heartbeat transforms auditory energy into a structured cycle—what we call a *period*. The moment sound becomes periodic, it ceases to be mere noise and becomes *information*.

## 2. The Delight of the Pattern

The human brain is fundamentally a prediction machine. We are constantly searching for order in our environment to anticipate what will happen next.

When we encounter a repeating pulse, our brains instantly lock onto this organised information. We recognise the pattern—we feel a "groove"—and unconsciously begin to project it forward. We anticipate the exact moment the next beat will arrive. This silent, internal game of prediction forms the bedrock of our connection to music.

## 3. The Joys of Listening

The deep pleasure we take in listening to music is intimately tied to this continuous cycle of expectation. It works in two distinct ways:

**The Payoff of the Expected:** There is a visceral satisfaction when a pattern resolves exactly as we predicted. When a heavy beat lands squarely in the pocket we anticipated, our brain's predictive model is validated. This gives us a powerful sense of order, stability, and resolution.

**The Thrill of the Unexpected:** Yet, if a pattern repeats endlessly without variation, like a metronome, we quickly tune it out. Music comes alive when a pattern establishes a strong expectation, only to intentionally subvert it. A rhythm might shift slightly, or a beat might land a fraction of a second early. This subversion forces our brains to rapidly update our predictions. In that moment of surprise, we experience emotion, tension, and delight.

## 4. The Zoom Lens: Slow and Fast Patterns

These patterns are easy to recognise when they occur slowly enough for us to track them individually, like tapping our feet. We call this slow repetition *rhythm*.

But what happens if we speed that pattern up? Our auditory system acts like a perceptual "zoom lens." If a repeating pulse accelerates past roughly 26 times per second (about 25.8Hz), our ears can no longer distinguish the individual beats. Instead, they fuse together into a continuous, sustained tone. We call this *pitch*.

A slow drum beat and a high-pitched whistle are physically identical phenomena: both are periodic patterns of energy over time. The only difference is the rate at which they occur. Rhythm and pitch are the exact same structural reality, just experienced at different perceptual speeds.

## 5. Giving the Patterns a Name

If music is fundamentally about organising, anticipating, and subverting repeating patterns of energy across time, we need a precise way to describe these phenomena.

This is the purpose of Prime Period Theory. It is not a rigid set of rules dictating how music should be made; rather, it is a unified vocabulary for describing periodic shapes and relationships. Just as geometry gives painters a shared language for space and form, Prime Period Theory gives us a shared framework to describe the architecture of time—from the microscopic texture of a single sustained note to the overarching rhythm of a complete song.
