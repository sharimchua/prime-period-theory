title: Patterns in Time
description: An introduction to music as information and the psychology of expectation.

If we want to understand how music works on a structural level, we have to start by forgetting about notes, chords, and scales for a moment. We need to start with first principles: the physical reality of sound, and the psychology of how we listen to it.

## 1. The Raw Material: Sound vs Information

Imagine the chaotic din of a busy street, the rustle of leaves in the wind, or the crackle of television static. This is sound—energy moving through the air—but it is erratic and unpredictable. To our brains, it is essentially just noise.

But what happens when a sound starts to repeat? Think of a dripping faucet, a ticking clock, or the steady thud of a heartbeat. The moment that disorganized energy organizes into a repeating cycle—what we call a *period*—it undergoes a massive transformation. It stops being random noise and becomes *information*.

## 2. The Delight of the Pattern

Why are we so drawn to these patterns? Because the human brain is, at its core, a relentless prediction machine. We are constantly scanning our surroundings, trying to find order and figure out what is going to happen next.

When we hear a repeating pulse, our brains immediately latch onto it. We recognize the pattern—we feel a "groove"—and unconsciously project it forward in time. We know exactly when the next beat is going to land. This internal game of prediction and anticipation is the absolute foundation of how we experience music.

## 3. The Joys of Listening

The reason listening to music feels so incredibly good is deeply tied to this ongoing cycle of expectation. It works in two main ways:

**The Payoff of the Expected:** There is a deep, physical satisfaction when a pattern plays out exactly as we predicted. When a heavy beat lands perfectly where we expected it, our brain's predictive model of the world is validated. We feel a profound sense of stability, order, and resolution.

**The Thrill of the Unexpected:** But if a pattern just repeats endlessly, like that ticking clock, we quickly get bored and tune it out. Music truly comes alive when it sets up a strong expectation and then intentionally subverts it. A beat might hit a split-second early, or a rhythm might suddenly shift. This momentary subversion forces our brains to instantly update our predictions. That tiny shock of surprise is what we register as emotion, tension, and delight.

## 4. The Zoom Lens: Slow and Fast Patterns

These patterns are easy to spot when they happen slowly enough for us to tap our foot to them. We typically call this slow repetition *rhythm*.

But what happens if you take a repeating pattern and speed it up incredibly fast? Our brains have a built-in perceptual "zoom lens." If a beat repeats more than about 26 times a second, our ears can no longer track the individual thuds. Instead, they fuse together into a single, continuous sensation. We call this *pitch*.

A slow drumbeat and a high, sustained whistle are actually the exact same physical thing: repeating patterns of energy over time. The only difference is the speed. Rhythm and pitch are simply the same underlying phenomenon experienced at different timescales!

## 5. Giving the Patterns a Name

If music is fundamentally about organizing, anticipating, and subverting repeating patterns across time, we need a clear way to talk about it.

This is where Prime Period Theory comes in. It isn't a strict set of rules about how to write a song. Instead, it’s simply a vocabulary for describing these periodic shapes and patterns. Just as geometry gives a painter a language to talk about perspective and form, Prime Period Theory gives us a shared framework to describe the architecture of time—unifying our understanding of everything from a single microscopic sound wave to the sweeping rhythm of a song.
