title: Patterns in Time
description: An introduction to music as information and the psychology of expectation.

If we want to understand how music works on a structural level, we have to start by forgetting about notes, chords, and scales for a moment. We need to start with first principles: the physical reality of sound, and the psychology of how we listen to it.

## 1. The Raw Material: Sound vs Information

Music starts with amplitude over time—physical energy vibrating through the air. However, a single crash or the random crackle of static provides very little for our brains to hold onto. It is, essentially, just noise.

When auditory energy organises into a repeating cycle—a steady pulse or a consistent wave—it stops being noise and transforms into *information*. This repeating cycle is what we call a *period*. Once a sound becomes periodic, our brains can begin to track it.

## 2. The Delight of the Pattern

Why are we so drawn to these repeating cycles? The human brain is fundamentally a prediction machine, constantly seeking order in our environment. When we hear a periodic sound, a pattern is established.

Once this pattern is recognised, we unconsciously start projecting it forward in time. We anticipate when the next beat will land or when the waveform will repeat. At a slow speed, we often feel this as a "groove." This internal, silent game of predicting the future is the foundation of our entire relationship with music.

## 3. The Joys of Listening

The reason music feels good to listen to is deeply tied to this cycle of expectation. Our aesthetic experience of music operates on two main axes:

**The Payoff of the Expected:** When a pattern resolves exactly as we predicted—like a heavy downbeat landing right in the pocket or a harmony reaching a shared period—it provides a visceral payoff. Our brain's model of the world is confirmed. We experience the joy of stability, recognition, and resolution.

**The Thrill of the Unexpected:** But if music only ever fulfilled expectations, it would be as boring as a ticking clock. The real power of music happens when a strong expectation is set, and then intentionally subverted or delayed. A beat might hit a fraction of a second early, or a pitch might shift unexpectedly. This forces our brains to instantly update our predictive model, and that momentary surprise registers as emotional weight, tension, and delight.

## 4. The Zoom Lens: Slow and Fast Patterns

The fascinating thing about these patterns is that they are scale-invariant. Our brains possess a perceptual boundary that acts like a zoom lens.

When a periodic pattern happens slowly—fewer than about 26 repetitions per second—we hear the individual events. This is what we call *rhythm*.

If you speed that exact same pattern up so it repeats faster than 26 times per second, our ears can no longer track the separate beats. They fuse together into a single, continuous sensation. This is what we call *pitch*.

A slow drum beat and a sustained, high-pitched whistle are not different phenomena. They are the exact same physical structure—a repeating period of amplitude—just operating at different speeds. The underlying mechanics of expectation and resolution are identical.

## 5. Giving the Patterns a Name

Since pitch, harmony, rhythm, and timbre all share the same underlying structure, we need a common language to talk about them.

This is where Prime Period Theory comes in. It is not a set of rules for how to make music. It is simply a vocabulary for describing these periodic shapes and patterns. Just as geometry gives a painter a language for perspective and form, Prime Period Theory provides a shared toolkit to describe the architecture of time, whether we are zooming in on a microscopic pitch or zooming out to the rhythm of a song.
